Stochastic Parrots

Language models sound shockingly human.
But do they understand a single word — and what does their sheer size cost us?

The great AI size race

Since 2018, AI research has been locked in a race to build ever larger language models — systems trained to predict the next word in a sequence.

Each bubble is a real model. Its size: how many parameters it has. Watch what happens in just three years.

4,600× in two years

From BERT (340 million parameters) to GPT-3 (175 billion) to Switch-C (1.6 trillion).

For scale: the human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons. GPT-3 alone has twice that many parameters.

The chart uses a logarithmic scale — otherwise Switch-C would tower so high it would drown out everything else. That explosive leap is the story.

Meet the stochastic parrot

Here is the uncomfortable truth: these models do not know what words mean. They have no intent, no thoughts, no grasp of the world.

They are, in the authors' words, stochastic parrots: they haphazardly stitch together sequences of words based on statistical probabilities learned from training data.

Nothing more.

Try it yourself

On the right, the parrot is completing a sentence the only way it can: by guessing the most probable next word.

Click a candidate to accept it — and watch it pick the next one. It never chooses because a word is true, only because it is likely.

Probabilities are illustrative, not measured from the paper.

The illusion of meaning

So why does it feel like the machine understands? Because you supply the meaning.

Humans are built to read intent into fluent language. The coherence is real — but it exists only in your head, not in the model.

That gap is where the dangers of this technology begin.

The hidden environmental & climate costs

Every response has a footprint.

Behind a model's fluent words are computers processing data in data centres. Developing, training and using a model all require electricity.

Producing that electricity can release greenhouse gases. How much depends on the hardware, where and when it runs, and the electricity used.

The paper asks us to consider these costs alongside the benefits of language technology.

The appetite for compute

The paper reports that the computing used to train the largest deep-learning models increased 300,000× in six years.

How big is a footprint?

The paper reports an estimated 284 tonnes of CO₂ for one Transformer development procedure that included trying different model designs. This historical estimate is not the footprint of every model.

For context, the paper also reports a global average of 5 tonnes CO₂e per person per year in 2017. Scroll to see what happens when that annual amount is added up over time.

This is an illustration that holds the 2017 average constant, not a prediction. CO₂e expresses the warming effect of greenhouse gases as an equivalent amount of CO₂. Because the paper labels the two figures differently, their crossing is not an exact emissions equivalence.

Source: Bender et al. (2021), §3, p. 612

The whole lifecycle

Training isn't the end.

Building a model can involve many experiments and retraining runs. Once deployed, the model needs computing power every time it is used. This is called inference.

The paper notes that, over a model's lifetime, the combined cost of repeated use may exceed its training cost. The balance depends on the model and how often it is used.

For another training example, the paper reports that one BERT base training run on GPUs, without hyperparameter tuning, used roughly as much energy as a trans-American flight. This is a comparison of energy, not a count of flights or a CO₂ estimate.

Source: Bender et al. (2021), §3

The cost of improvement

What is a small gain worth?

Better results can be valuable, but they may also require substantial resources. The paper cites one English-to-German translation experiment that gained 0.1 BLEU—a translation benchmark score—at an estimated additional compute cost of $150,000.

This is one historical example, not a universal cost. Expensive computing can also make it harder for under-resourced researchers to participate.

Source: Bender et al. (2021), §3, p. 612

Climate justice

Who bears the costs?

The authors ask who experiences climate risks and who benefits from language technology. Sudan and the Maldives are two examples they discuss.

They contrast these risks with language models primarily developed for English, while comparable large models for Sudanese Arabic and Dhivehi were lacking at the time.

Rotate the globe as you scroll, or use its controls to explore. The paper does not rank countries by costs or benefits.

Source: Bender et al. (2021), §3, pp. 612–613

The internet is not the world

To get big, models swallow the web. But the web is not a representative sample of humanity.

67% of US Reddit users are men. Only 8.8–15% of Wikipedia editors are women. And more than 90% of the world's languages have virtually no language technology at all.

Filtered silence

To "clean" the data, developers filter it — for example with lists of ~400 "bad words".

But those lists are overwhelmingly sexual terms, so they also erase the language of marginalised communities — like LGBTQ+ spaces that use reclaimed words. The filter silences the very voices the data was already missing.

And at terabyte scale, nobody documents what is actually inside: documentation debt — harms you can no longer audit or fix.

Fluent ≠ harmless

Because we trust smooth language, synthetic text causes real harm: automated propaganda, extremist recruitment at scale — and AI errors that people act on without questioning.

Three cases show how it goes wrong.

Three failures, one root cause

1 · Wrongful arrest — a translation app turned "good morning" into "attack them"; the speaker was arrested.

2 · Bot propaganda — models generate endless plausible conspiracy posts, faking human crowds.

3 · Privacy leaks — models memorise training data and can regurgitate personal information.

Different failures, one root: form without meaning, at scale.

What can we do?

The authors' answer is not "bigger", but smarter and fairer:

Unchecked scalingCurated & sustainable AI
Ingest everything on the webCollect only what can be documented and vetted
Bigger = betterMeasure energy and cost, not just accuracy ("Green AI")
Build first, fix laterPre-mortems & affected communities in the design
