Stochastic Parrots

Language models have become shockingly good at sounding human.
But can they really think — and at what cost do they get so big?

scroll to begin ↓

The parrot that fools everyone

Meet the stochastic parrot — a machine that stitches together words and sentences it has memorised, fluently and confidently, but without any grasp of what it is saying.

It sounds human. It is not thinking.

2 years. 4,600× bigger.

Between 2019 and 2021, English language models grew at a staggering pace — in both parameters (how "big" the model is) and the amount of text they were trained on.

From BERT (340 million parameters) to GPT-3 (175 billion) to Switch-C (1.6 trillion), the field was in a literal arms race.

The chart below uses a logarithmic scale — otherwise GPT-3 and Switch-C would tower so high they'd drown out everything else. That oversized leap is the story.

How big is "too big"?

Each bubble is a model. Its size shows parameter count; its colour shows how much training text it used.

Watch the timeline advance: model after model, the bubbles inflate — while the datasets behind them swell too.

For comparison, the average human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons. GPT-3 alone has twice that many parameters.

The hidden price tag

That runaway growth doesn't come cheap.

Training a single large model can emit as much CO₂ as an entire lifetime's worth of emissions for ~57 people — and training one BERT uses as much energy as a trans-Atlantic flight.

Efficiency improvements grow far more slowly than model sizes. Bigger models = disproportionately bigger bills.

Scoring higher, spending more

Even the performance gains are expensive. In machine translation, pushing a model just a tiny bit further — gains of 0.1 BLEU on a score that runs to 100 — can cost around $150,000 in compute.

That is a lot of electricity for a nearly imperceptible improvement.

But wait — what's inside?

Big models need big data. So developers scrape the entire internet. And the internet is not the world.

If you train on everything on the web, you also swallow its biases: whose voices are there, and whose are missing?

The one-sided internet

The people who shape the data are overwhelmingly not representative of humanity.

Reddit is dominated by young men. Wikipedia's editors are mostly men. So the "world" a language model learns is skewed from the start.

Filtered silence

Filters may screen out hateful words — but they also wash away marginalised communities.

A "bad words" list used to clean web data overwhelmingly flags sexual language, deleting words like "twink" — and with it, the voice of LGBTQ+ spaces that use that language.

So fluent. So hollow.

Even with all this data, the parrot doesn't understand. It predicts the next likely word — nothing more.

When it produces text that looks coherent, we humans are the ones supplying the meaning. That is exactly what makes it dangerous.

What can we do?

The researchers' verdict: don't just scale up bigger and bigger. Curation beats consumption — curate smaller, documented datasets rather than swallowing the whole internet blindly.

And measure success not only by accuracy, but by cost and efficiency.
